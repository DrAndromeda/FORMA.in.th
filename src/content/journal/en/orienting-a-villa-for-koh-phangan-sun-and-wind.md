A villa's comfort on a tropical island is decided long before anyone chooses a finish or a piece of furniture. It is decided in the first week of design, when massing, glazing and roof geometry are set against the specific way sun and wind move across a given plot. Get this stage wrong and no amount of air conditioning fully compensates for it; get it right and mechanical cooling becomes a backup rather than a necessity.

## Solar path first, view second

It is tempting to orient a villa purely toward the best view — the sea, a valley, a sunset horizon — and treat solar exposure as a problem to solve afterward with tinted glass or heavier air conditioning. On Koh Phangan, where the sun sits high and intense for much of the year, that ordering usually produces a home that is beautiful in photographs and uncomfortable to actually inhabit through the middle of the day.

A better sequence studies solar path across the seasons first, then finds the view orientation that can be achieved within that constraint — often by stepping the building, angling specific rooms independently of the overall site orientation, or using deep overhangs and secondary shading elements to reconcile the two. Sightlines and comfort are not opposing goals; they simply need to be resolved together rather than sequentially.

## Wind is a design material, not an afterthought

Cross-ventilation only works if a building's plan actually creates a clear path for air to move through it. This means opposing openings at a similar height, avoiding solid internal partitions that dead-end an air path, and, where possible, using stack-effect voids — a double-height space or light well — to draw warm air up and out rather than letting it pool at ceiling level.

On exposed coastal sites, the opposite problem can occur: wind that is strong enough to be uncomfortable or to drive rain deep into covered terraces. Here, partial screening, angled overhangs and secondary wind-breaks tuned to the specific site's exposure matter more than a generic openness principle applied uniformly everywhere.

## Shading geometry deserves the same rigour as the floor plan

Overhangs, louvres and secondary roofs are often treated as a finishing touch, added once the main design is resolved. Calculated properly, however, shading geometry is closely tied to a site's latitude and the specific months a client most wants to use particular outdoor spaces — a north-facing terrace and a west-facing terrace need genuinely different overhang depths and angles to perform equally well.

## Practical implications for a Koh Phangan brief

For most sites we work with, this translates into a short list of concrete design moves: orient primary living spaces to capture prevailing sea breeze rather than solely the most photogenic view; use deep, calculated overhangs on west-facing glazing in particular, where afternoon sun is most intense; and treat any solid perimeter wall as a last resort for privacy, since it usually blocks the airflow the rest of the design depends on.

None of this requires an unusual or experimental architectural style. It requires treating climate analysis as a genuine design input from day one, which is exactly how we begin every villa commission on the island.
